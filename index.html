The Traveling Exhibition of
Electrical Wonders

In the late 1800s, science was entertainment. Traveling lecturers packed theaters with audiences eager to see electricity tamed and put on display — sparks, glowing tubes, bodies wired up like circuits. It was part science fair, part magic show.

One of the most popular attractions was the cathode ray tube: a sealed glass tube, most of its air pumped out, with two metal plates sealed inside — a cathode, wired to the negative side of a power source, and an anode, wired to the positive side. Switch on the power, and a glowing beam shoots from the cathode all the way across the tube to the anode.

Notice: the wire only connects the battery to each plate. Between the cathode and anode, inside the tube, there's nothing but empty space — yet the beam crosses it.

Two things about this beam confounded scientists.

First — electricity without a wire. Every circuit anyone had ever built moved electricity along a continuous metal path, start to finish. But inside this tube, the beam crossed open, empty space with no wire connecting the two plates at all. If electricity could move through nothing, what did that actually mean about what electricity was? Could a form of power someday travel through open air — into a room, a building — without a single wire strung between?

Second — where was the beam coming from? The beam appeared to start right at the cathode's metal surface. Could the beam somehow be made of tiny pieces of that very metal, breaking free and flying across the tube? If so, that would mean atoms — supposedly the smallest, most "uncuttable" unit of matter — weren't so uncuttable after all.

Nobody knew for certain. J.J. Thomson set out to find out — with three experiments. Explore each one below. Drag, toggle, and test things yourself before you find out what Thomson actually discovered.

Exhibit No. 1

Does the Ray Carry Charge?

● Insert image or GIF here
suggested: a diagram or photo of an electrometer, or the real apparatus from Thomson's first experiment

Thomson aimed the cathode ray at a device called an electrometer, which detects electric charge. He rigged up a magnet so he could bend the ray away from the electrometer's opening whenever he chose.

Try it: drag the magnet closer to (or farther from) the beam. Watch what happens to both the beam and the electrometer reading.

Drag the magnet — closer bends the beam more. Flip it to reverse which way the beam bends.

When the beam reached the electrometer's opening undisturbed, the device registered a strong negative charge. When Thomson bent the beam away with a magnet, the charge reading disappeared too.

Conclusion: The negative charge and the beam can't be separated — they travel together. Whatever this ray is, it carries charge with it.

Exhibit No. 2

Can an Electric Field Push the Ray?

If the cathode ray really is made of charged particles, it should be pushed off course by an electric field — the same way a charged balloon gets pulled toward (or pushed away from) a charged object.

Yes — once Thomson removed nearly all the leftover gas from the tube, the beam bent through the electric field exactly the way a stream of charged particles should.

Conclusion: This confirmed the cathode ray really was made of charged particles — not light, and not some other kind of wave.

Exhibit No. 3

How Big Are These Particles?

Thomson used a magnet to measure how much force it took to push the beam off course. This let him compare the size of these mystery particles to the size of an atom — using hydrogen, the smallest known atom at the time, as the yardstick.

The particles turned out to be far smaller and lighter than even a hydrogen atom — more than 1,000 times smaller.

Conclusion: These particles were too small to be atoms themselves. They had to be tiny pieces of atoms — meaning atoms are not the smallest form of matter after all.

The Verdict

Based on all three exhibits, Thomson reached three conclusions

  • 1Cathode rays are made of tiny, negatively charged particles — we now call these electrons.
  • 2Electrons are parts of atoms, not separate substances.
  • 3Electrons can move — they aren't locked in place.

This evidence led Thomson to propose a new model of the atom — one with internal structure, electrons embedded inside it: the Plum Pudding Model.
